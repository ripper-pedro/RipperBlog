Engineering loves being inspired by concepts from biology, even though that inspiration is often more about using a name merely as a metaphor for how something works, instead of actually trying to reproduce its mechanisms — see &#34;neurons&#34; and &#34;neural networks.&#34; One technique that has become popular recently reverses this logic: it imitates an old biological concept and does not use its name as marketing. I’m talking about Reinforcement Learning, the way we teach modern robots to move and interact with the world, going beyond classical control theory. Essentially, it works in a way similar to Pavlov’s dog: when the model gets closer to performing a task the way you want it to, it gets a cookie, and when it gets it wrong, it gets a shock.

For this project, I downloaded the ML Agents repository for Unity, which contains several examples of entities that have to perform specific tasks in different scenarios. The fun part is that they learn to perform these tasks &#34;on their own.&#34; One of the examples that interested me the most, which I modified and trained from scratch, was the Crawler. It is basically a &#34;spider&#34; that has to learn how to walk. The original model consisted of a ball with four articulated &#34;legs&#34; made up of two segments (photo on the left). In other words, it has a femur and a tibia, and it can move either the whole leg or just the &#34;knee.&#34; What I did was add feet and ankles (photo on the right).

Training works like this: for each episode of the iteration, the network receives information from the Crawlers (yes, plural, because there are actually 10 learning in parallel) and chooses movements (initially at random), whose results are simulated by Unity so they can be rewarded or punished, depending on how close they got to a particular goal. This makes the next episode of movements tend to repeat and improve the ones that worked in the previous episode. Don’t worry if that sentence sounded long and confusing; I’ll explain it in more detail below.

At each decision, the agent receives a vector of numbers called an observation, which summarizes information such as linear and angular velocity, the position and orientation of each limb, the direction and distance to the target, and linear and angular acceleration, as well as the body’s distance from and contact with the ground. It is like a set of numerical sensors. The network can also control 28 actions by choosing continuous values for the desired angles of each joint and the force needed to reach those angles. In short, the network &#34;sees&#34; the model’s current state and dictates what it will do in the next state.

When a new episode begins, Unity simulates these actions and their physical consequences, updating the observation vector and receiving new actions afterward. The agent is reset if it falls or reaches the step limit for an episode, returning its body parts to their initial positions and choosing a new direction and target speed. In this example, there are two types of reward, each calculated from 0 to 1 and multiplied together at each physics step: how close the body’s average velocity is to the target speed and how well aligned the body is with the desired direction. Touching the target also increases the reward by +1.

The agent tends to repeat and improve actions that led to higher rewards because the optimization policy gradually adjusts the network’s weights to increase the likelihood of actions associated with better outcomes. So, at the beginning of training, its movements are completely random, causing it to fall frequently and just flail around (like in the first GIF). As time passes, the &#34;convulsions&#34; become more rhythmic and start going in the same direction. Then it actually starts controlling the limbs so they work together, and the next steps are about refining higher-level movements (like in the second GIF, where it is already walking but still has trouble turning).

After 10 million steps, the training stabilized with an average reward of around 3,000. The agent learned to control its four legs in a coordinated way to turn and walk directly to the goal, respecting the target speed.

{{< project-gallery layout="standard" folder="teaching-spider-to-walk" images="printlog.jpeg" alts="Screenshot of the final section of the training log" >}}

The current training, however, does not ask for a specific way of walking. There is no explicit reward for alternating the legs, keeping a rhythm, planting the foot without slipping, or reducing swaying. That is why it looks kind of &#34;ugly,&#34; walking crookedly and shakily instead of naturally and smoothly. The agent is learning that moving and reaching the targets is what matters, not the appearance of its gait. This could be improved by adding new types of rewards, tasks, or terrain. Another approach would be to use Imitation Learning, where it would be based on a pre-programmed animation, just to serve as a basis for a particular style, and learn to make this animation work while interacting with the physical world.

In the end, it seems to me that the Crawler with feet is a little more efficient than the original Crawler, especially when turning in another direction. Maybe that is just because the legs as a whole became a little longer, so it can move faster... Or maybe the feet, with their extra degrees of freedom, really do help.

This field is incredible and fascinates me a lot. It can be used to do much more interesting and complex things than teaching a virtual &#34;spider&#34; to walk in Unity. One of the first demonstrations to become famous for showing the power of Reinforcement Learning for embodied AI was that of the [Blue robot](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=YxH4Mx6zh6c), a kind of &#34;duck&#34; inspired by Star Wars. It was developed by Google DeepMind and Disney Research in partnership with NVIDIA and presented by NVIDIA’s then CEO, Jensen Huang — who knows what will happen now, right? — during the GTC Paris keynotes in 2025. It took the stage full of personality, with natural movements and realistic interaction. The audience was delighted, and its success was very likely what inspired the creation of the [Microduck](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=reiTh7K4KSc) — the similarities are undeniable — by Pollen Robotics, Hugging Face’s arm for Physical AI based in Bordeaux (Hugging Face, by the way, is being acquired by NVIDIA). This little robot is essentially a miniature, open-source, low-cost version of Blue, so researchers and students can experiment and learn.
